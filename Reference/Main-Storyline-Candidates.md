# Main Storyline Candidates

**Established hook (locked in):** climbing the CN Tower and reactivating its radio transmitter is played straight as the game's apparent main objective — a satisfying, iconic setpiece that primes the player to believe they've won. It is a **false victory**: completing it doesn't end the game, it reveals that the real objective is something else entirely. What that something else is remains open — this file lays out four candidate directions, each fully detailed, plus a proposal for combining all four into one structure rather than picking just one.

**Structural clarification, established via analogy to Fallout: New Vegas's own critical path (see `to-be-integrated/New_Vegas_plot-beat_reference.txt` — a general beat-by-beat FNV reference kept on hand for exactly this kind of comparison):** the CN Tower is **not** the player's very first goal — it's a landmark that lands somewhere roughly around the middle-ish of the early game, loosely comparable to FNV's Novac/REPCONN Test Center beat (well past Goodsprings/Sunny Smiles, the Powder Gangers, Primm, the Mojave Outpost, and Nipton, but not itself the climax). The exact position isn't meant to be pinned to a precise beat count — just "somewhere in that middle stretch," not the opening. That means there's a genuinely separate, **not-yet-decided "immediate-immediate-immediate" goal** that comes before the CN Tower — the Goodsprings-and-Doc-Mitchell-equivalent hook ("find the guy who shot you," in FNV's case) that organically leads the player toward wanting to climb the tower in the first place. This earlier hook is open territory, distinct from both the CN Tower beat itself and the "real objective" revealed after its false victory (which would land further along the critical path, loosely analogous to arriving in Vegas and confronting Benny).

**Shared-universe placement — confirmed (2026-07-29):** Toronto Fell Out is real, load-bearing shared-universe canon, not just loosely adjacent to it. The shared timeline's own reference map (`media/Reference/TepenianUniverseTimeline`) already shows a **"DMZ strip near the Great Lakes"** among the post-2083 North American successor polities — explicitly not a nation, no signatory status, previously undeveloped. **Toronto Fell Out is that DMZ.** The game takes place in the shared timeline's "Set-Up" era (~2088–2131) — Toronto's 2097 setting is shortly after the September 26, 2083 global nuclear war, not centuries after it. **The Falkland Treaty (2564) and everything downstream of it — Antarctic exile, sentient robots at large in society, Concordia, Zodiac districts, the Long Night War — is roughly 500 years in TFO's future and has no bearing on this game.** See `Worldspace/World_History_Reference.md` for the full placement detail.

**Confirmed consequence — Fallout's radiation-era material genuinely applies, not just as borrowed flavor:** because Toronto Fell Out is a real post-nuclear-war setting within the shared timeline (not a separate, self-contained fictional wasteland), ghouls, radiation resistance/exposure mechanics, and the whole cluster of radiation-themed perks and traits already in `Game-Mechanics/Perks/Perks.md` and `Game-Mechanics/Character-Creation/Traits.md` (Rad Child, Rad Resistance, Rad Absorption, Irradiated Beauty, Atomic!, Lead Belly, etc.) are canonically accurate to this game's actual setting, not just Fallout-homage reskins.

**Confirmed mechanic — the map's edges ARE the quarantine, not a separate boundary system.** The player-facing implementation of "the perimeter" is the open world's actual map boundary: there's no invisible wall, no impassable mountain range, no fog-of-death zone. If the player tries to leave the play area, they get shot at by surrounding perimeter checkpoints. The world edge and the central story's quarantine premise are the same thing, mechanically and narratively.

## 1. It's Not a Rescue Signal — It's a Quarantine Perimeter

**The reveal**: reactivating the tower successfully reconnects Toronto to the outside world. The response isn't rescue. It's confirmation that Toronto was **deliberately sealed off** after whatever caused the "Fell Out," and whoever's still out there — a remnant Canadian government, a military containment authority, an international body, something else TBD — has no intention of opening the perimeter back up. The player's moment of triumph (*we're not alone, someone's out there*) curdles immediately into something worse: *someone's out there, and they already decided we don't get to leave.*

**Why it fits this game specifically**: it's the option that makes the title itself part of the mystery. "Toronto Fell Out" already reads two ways — radioactive fallout, and a falling-out, a rupture between people. This direction makes that second meaning literal at a geopolitical scale: Toronto didn't just fall out environmentally, it fell out *of the world's good graces*, and finding out why becomes the emotional core of the game.

**What it sets up**:
- A real, external antagonist force (the quarantine authority) that doesn't need to be physically present to be a constant pressure — its enforcement (aerial patrols, jamming, a hard perimeter, propaganda drops) can be felt throughout without requiring a traditional "final boss faction" on the ground.
- A natural central question for the whole game: *why specifically Toronto?* — which can stay a slow-burn mystery resolved only at the endgame.
- A strong thematic hook for whatever hidden system tracks the player's impact on the city (if Cohesion is chosen: can a fractured, quarantined city cohere enough to force its way back into the world, or does it fall further apart under the pressure of being sealed off).
- **Confirmed mechanical realization**: the quarantine perimeter *is* the game's actual map boundary. Approaching the world's edge means encountering the perimeter directly (checkpoints, patrols, getting shot at) rather than an arbitrary invisible wall — the "you can't leave" of the open-world genre and the "you can't leave" of the story are literally the same thing.

**Narrowed by the shared-universe placement**: Toronto Fell Out is the shared timeline's own "DMZ strip near the Great Lakes" — already established as a real, if previously undeveloped, non-nation buffer zone on the post-2083 North American map. That gives "who's enforcing the quarantine" a concrete starting shortlist rather than a blank slate: whichever successor polities actually border the Great Lakes region (Quebec and the People's Democratic Republic of New England are the most geographically obvious candidates from the confirmed roster; the Midwest Republic is also plausible depending on exact DMZ extent) are the natural candidates for who polices it, jointly or otherwise. Exact identity and motive are still open — see the dedicated TODO item — but this is a real narrowing, not a blank "TBD."

**Open questions**: Which specific bordering polity (or polities) is actually enforcing the perimeter, and are they wrong to? (The most interesting version of this isn't "evil government conspiracy," it's "they have an actual reason, and it's at least partly justified" — see Option 3.) Is the endgame about breaking the quarantine, exposing it publicly, negotiating with it, or something else?

## 2. The Signal Doesn't Call for Help — It Wakes Something Up

**The reveal**: Toronto had its own automated civil-infrastructure system — the same category of tech that produces the game's Securitron-/Sentry-Bot-style automated defenses, explicitly *not* sentient, just very literal-minded pre-war automation — and it's been sitting dormant since the disaster. The CN Tower broadcast at full power is exactly the trigger signal it was built to wait for. Reactivating the tower doesn't just reconnect Toronto to the outside world; it also flips this system back on, mid-execution of decades-old standing orders that were never updated for a world where the city's survivors are civilians rather than "anomalies to be cleared."

**Why it fits this game specifically**: it uses tech and enemy types you've already committed to (automated defenses, no sentient robots) as a genuine plot engine rather than just combat flavor, and it sidesteps the "the AI is secretly evil" cliché — the horror here is that it's *not* evil, it's just doing exactly what it was told to do a long time ago, and nobody told it the war ended.

**What it sets up**:
- A ticking-clock, actively-dangerous-right-now threat distinct from any slower political conflict — good pacing counterweight if paired with a subtler, slower-burn option like #1.
- A forced-cooperation scenario: factions who otherwise hate each other may need to deal with a common mechanical threat, whether they actually cooperate or just deal with it in parallel.
- A recurring "field-by-field" structure — the system doesn't have to wake up everywhere at once, it can activate district-by-district, giving you a natural way to escalate stakes across the whole map rather than dumping the whole threat in one place.

**Open questions**: What were the system's original standing orders, and why were they so aggressive? (This connects naturally to Option 3 — a system doesn't get built with "cleanse the area of anomalies" instructions unless it was protecting something specific.) Can it be reasoned with/reprogrammed, or is it purely an obstacle to survive and shut down?

## 3. The Signal Reveals What's Actually Buried Under the City

**The reveal**: the tower's real pre-war purpose ties to something underneath Toronto — a bunker, a reactor, a research site, an archive, something still undecided — and reactivating it doesn't just broadcast outward, it broadcasts *the coordinates* (or the existence, or a partial data-key) to every faction with the means to listen. The false victory here isn't "no one's coming to help," it's "everyone who matters just found out what you found out, at the same time you did."

**Why it fits this game specifically**: it's the option that most directly manufactures a New-Vegas-style multi-faction endgame — several factions racing toward the same prize, each with a different reason for wanting it and a different plan for what to do with it once they have it. It also gives Options 1 and 2 a *reason to exist*: something worth quarantining an entire city over, and worth building an aggressive automated defense system to protect.

**What it sets up**:
- A concrete, discoverable "why" underneath the more abstract political/mechanical threats in Options 1 and 2.
- Multiple factions with legitimately different stances on the buried asset (destroy it, control it, sell access to it, expose it publicly, use it as leverage with the quarantine authority) — strong material for branching endgame choices.
- A capstone location/dungeon that the whole game can build toward, in the CN-Tower-setpiece tradition established at the very start.

**Open questions**: What is actually buried down there? (Left deliberately open — a reactor, a bioweapon research site, a data archive explaining what really happened, a pre-war government facility, something else.) Does it need to be inherently dangerous, or can it be dangerous only because of who wants it?

## 4. You Weren't the First One to Find This Frequency

**The reveal**: the tower's frequency wasn't silent and waiting for a hero — someone's already been quietly using a limited or partial version of it: coordinating raids, running black-market logistics between districts, maybe even enforcing parts of a quarantine from the inside, or feeding information to whoever's outside the perimeter. Fully reactivating the tower at full power doesn't just reconnect Toronto to the world, it **steps on an existing operation** — jamming it, exposing it, or forcing whoever's been running it to react to suddenly losing exclusive control of the airwaves.

**Why it fits this game specifically**: it's the option that turns the "false victory" into an immediate, personal antagonist rather than an abstract political or mechanical one — someone was already benefiting from the status quo, and the player's actions just took that away from them. It's also the cheapest of the four to make land emotionally, since it can be revealed almost immediately (someone shows up, furious or scared, within the same scene).

**What it sets up**:
- An antagonist (individual or faction) who is reactive rather than a pre-existing "big bad" — they didn't plan to be the villain of this story, the player made them one by reactivating the tower.
- A natural investigative thread: *who were they talking to, and why does it matter that they stop?*
- The strongest option for a fast, early second-act hook, since it doesn't require worldbuilding a whole external government (Option 1) or a whole buried mystery (Option 3) to pay off — it can resolve at a personal/faction scale first, then widen out.

**Open questions**: Is this faction friend, foe, or something more complicated (sympathetic people doing a bad thing for an understandable reason)? Are they working *for* the quarantine authority, *against* it, or entirely unrelated to it?

## Combining All Four Into One Structure

These aren't actually mutually exclusive — a single irreversible action (broadcasting at full power from the tallest structure in the city) plausibly has more than one simultaneous consequence. Here's one way all four nest together as a single throughline rather than four competing options:

**The moment of reactivation triggers three things at once, not one:**
1. The signal reaches the outside world → sets up **Option 1** (the quarantine reveal) as the slow-burn political mystery running underneath the whole game.
2. The signal is exactly the trigger the city's automated custodian system was waiting for → sets up **Option 2** (the awakened defense network) as the immediate, escalating mechanical threat that keeps pressure on the player in the short term.
3. The signal collides with/overrides an existing, weaker signal already in use → sets up **Option 4** (the frequency squatter) as the first concrete, personal-scale antagonist the player actually meets and can interrogate.

**The unifying secret, revealed gradually across the game, is Option 3**: everything traces back to something specific buried under Toronto.
- It's *why* the quarantine authority sealed the city in the first place (Option 1's motive).
- It's *what* the automated custodian system was originally built to protect, which explains why its standing orders were so aggressive (Option 2's motive).
- It's *what* the frequency-squatting faction already knows about and has been quietly exploiting, guarding, or selling access to from the inside (Option 4's motive) — meaning they're not an unrelated third party, they're the internal arm of the same overall situation, whether as collaborators with the quarantine authority, opportunists profiting off the secret, or a resistance faction trying to protect it from being found by anyone else.

**Resulting act structure (draft)**:
- **Act 1**: Climb the tower, reactivate the radio. False victory. All three consequences hit at once or in quick succession.
- **Act 2**: The three threads develop in parallel — investigating the quarantine (political/mystery), surviving and pushing back the awakened defense network (action/urgency), and dealing with the exposed faction on the ground (personal/faction-scale antagonist, and the most direct source of concrete leads).
- **Act 3**: All three threads converge on the buried asset. The endgame becomes a multi-faction negotiation/race over what it is and what happens to it, with the quarantine authority, the awakened system (if not yet resolved), and whatever remains of the frequency-squatting faction all having a stake in the outcome — plus the player's own choice about whether to destroy it, control it, expose it to the world, or use it as leverage to end the quarantine.

This keeps every option's strongest quality — Option 1's thematic weight, Option 2's mechanical urgency, Option 3's concrete endgame payoff, Option 4's fast personal hook — without needing to discard any of them.

**Still fully open**: what the buried asset actually is, who the quarantine authority actually is, what the automated system's original purpose/orders were, and who the frequency-squatting faction turns out to be. None of these need to be decided to lock in the structure above, but all four will need answers eventually.
