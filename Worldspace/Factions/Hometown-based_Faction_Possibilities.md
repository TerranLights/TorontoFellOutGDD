# Geography is Character — A Faction/Community Derivation Technique

**Provenance note:** adapted from Inner Tepenia's "Suggested Collective Groupings from Tepenian City Refugee Backgrounds" document. That file's actual content (affinity groups derived from the lived geography of specific fictional Antarctic research-station cities — extreme inland cold, coastal/maritime access, logistical hub life, and so on) is 100% not applicable to Toronto Fell Out. What's preserved here is the underlying *methodology* — a transferable design technique for deriving organic community identity from lived environmental and occupational history, rather than from power structures or faction politics.

**This file does not name, finalize, or invent any actual Toronto Fell Out faction.** Toronto Fell Out has zero designed districts and zero designed factions — this technique is documented now so it's ready to apply once that design work actually begins, not to pre-empt it.

---

## The Core Premise

**"Geography is Character."** A place develops a distinct collective psychology because of the specific, concrete conditions people lived under there — not because of an assigned faction alignment, but because of climate, daily labor, population density, degree of isolation or connectedness, and shared history. When people from a specific kind of place end up displaced, thrown together, or simply still living in that place after a catastrophe, they carry those lived patterns with them — and naturally cluster around what feels familiar, useful, and psychologically load-bearing.

**Why this matters for faction/community design specifically:** a faction that exists because "someone decided to found an organization with a mission statement" reads very differently from a community that exists because "this is just what happens when a few hundred people who all grew up doing the same kind of work under the same kind of pressure end up in the same place afterward." The second kind feels discovered rather than authored — which is exactly the quality Toronto Fell Out's own factions should aim for once they're designed, per the project's broader design philosophy of avoiding invented lore that doesn't feel load-bearing.

## How the Technique Works

For any given lived-environment category, ask the same three questions:

1. **What did daily survival actually require here, physically and socially?** Not the abstract theme of the place — the concrete daily texture of it. Long solitary shifts? Constant close-quarters cooperation? A specific kind of scarcity? A specific kind of danger?
2. **What belief system or life-principle would that daily texture plausibly produce, if it were lived for years or generations?** This should read as something a person from that background would say about themselves, not a slogan invented from outside.
3. **What would this group naturally *do* together once displaced or otherwise thrown into contact with people from different backgrounds?** Mutual aid, skill-sharing, remembrance practice, information networks, a specific communal ritual — something concrete, not just "they hang out."

A good result names: a plausible collective name (often with an alternate/informal variant), the core belief system in a sentence or two, the group's typical focus, and one or two concrete common expressions of that focus (a gathering type, a ritual, a mutual-aid pattern).

## Applying the Technique at a Meta Level to Toronto (Illustrative Only)

The categories below are worked examples of the *technique*, not proposals for actual Toronto Fell Out factions. They demonstrate what kind of group psychology this method of thinking tends to surface when pointed at real pre-collapse Toronto neighborhood/occupational archetypes in the abstract — nothing here should be read as decided, named, or claimed for the eventual real faction-design pass.

**Example category: people from a dense, high-rise residential core (e.g., a downtown condo-tower corridor).** Daily pre-collapse life here plausibly involved vertical living, reliance on shared building infrastructure (elevators, building management, utilities), and a level of anonymity among neighbors offset by occasional building-wide crisis cooperation (a shared power outage, a shared evacuation). A group formed from this background might plausibly value practical building-scale cooperation over broad political organizing, and might express that through skill-sharing specifically about making a single reclaimed structure livable — the same instinct that got them through a blackout now applied to surviving in the ruins of one.

**Example category: people from an industrial/logistics-heavy periphery (e.g., a rail yard, warehouse district, or port lands worker population).** Daily life here plausibly emphasized shift work, physical labor, redundancy planning, and pride in keeping a complex system running reliably. A group formed from this background might plausibly organize around maintenance-as-identity — the conviction that keeping something essential running for other people, without recognition, is the most legitimate form of contribution — expressed through practical workshops and quiet, unglamorous infrastructure work rather than public leadership.

**Example category: people from a specific ethnic or immigrant-heritage neighborhood with a strong existing mutual-aid tradition (Toronto has many real historical examples of this pattern).** Daily pre-collapse life here plausibly already included community networks built for newcomer integration, shared cultural practice, and collective care predating the collapse by generations. A group formed from this background wouldn't need to invent a mutual-aid ethic from scratch after the Fall Out — it would already have one, simply redirected toward a harsher post-collapse context. This is arguably the single richest category to draw from once real Toronto neighborhood research is done, precisely because it doesn't require inventing anything — it requires researching what was actually already there.

**Example category: people from an outdoor-recreation-adjacent or ravine/park-fringe area (Toronto's ravine system is geographically extensive and distinctive).** Daily life here plausibly involved comfort with green/wild spaces embedded inside a dense urban environment, informal trail knowledge, and a mindset already halfway toward "the city and the wild are not actually separate things." A group formed from this background might plausibly become the natural bridge population between ruined urban districts and whatever wilder, overgrown spaces exist in Toronto Fell Out's version of the city — not because they were assigned that role, but because they already knew the terrain before anyone had to.
